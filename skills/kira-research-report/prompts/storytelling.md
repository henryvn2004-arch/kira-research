# storytelling.md — the narrative arc and the hook (all routes; required for BRAIN)

A KIRA report is read by a time-poor strategy lead who decides in the first three pages whether to keep reading. It has to work three ways:
- **Skim:** the page titles alone, read in order, tell the whole story.
- **Scan:** each page's exhibit proves its title in five seconds.
- **Read:** the commentary rewards the reader with the "why" and the "so what".

This prompt sets the arc, the page grammar and the hook techniques. `voice_guide.md` still governs register and vocabulary. Where the two differ on length, this file wins.

## 1. Where this comes from

These are the working rules of strategy-firm decks and good business journalism, condensed:
- **Pyramid principle / SCQA:** answer first, then the arguments, then the evidence. Situation → Complication → Question → Answer.
- **Action titles and horizontal logic:** every title is a full-sentence conclusion, and the titles read in sequence form an argument.
- **Duarte's "what is / what could be":** tension between the status quo and a better future keeps attention.
- **The Heaths' "made to stick":** unexpected, concrete, credible, with stakes.
- **The journalist's lede and nut graf:** open on the most surprising true thing, then say why it matters now.

## 2. The report arc

| Act | Pages | Job | Device |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hook** | Exec summary, page 1 | Make the reader need the rest | Open on the tension: the surprising number or contradiction at the heart of the question. Then give the answer in one line and the three reasons. |
| **Situation** | First chapter | Shared ground, fast | Only what the reader must accept before the complication lands. Big numbers, few pages. |
| **Complication** | 1–2 chapters | Why the obvious move fails | The twist: the channel that looks dominant but is stalling, the margin that disappears, the rule that changes the maths. Spend the most pages here. |
| **Resolution** | 1–2 chapters | The way through | Options compared, the chosen route, why it wins. |
| **Proof of action** | Decision chapter | Make it executable | Conditions to win, risks, stage gates and budgets, first 90 days. |

- **Chapters:** each one opens with a divider. The divider's title is the chapter's claim, not a topic label ("The shelf is moving to the sari-sari store", not "Channel structure"), and its thesis line sets up the tension the chapter resolves.
- **Pages:** each page is a mini-arc (title = claim → exhibit = proof → commentary = why / nuance → takeaway = so what).
- **Open loops:** end a chapter's last takeaway with the consequence that the next chapter answers. This is a statement, not a question: "That shift has a price: the margin moves with it."
- **Rhythm:** alternate dense pages (table, matrix) with breather pages (a `big_number` hero or one bold chart). Never put three text-heavy pages in a row.

## 3. Page grammar (exhibit pages)

1. **Action title** (≤ 2 lines, sentence case, ends without a period unless it is a short thesis). It is the conclusion, with a number when one carries it.
   - ✅ "Sari-sari stores gained four share points while supermarkets stalled"
   - ❌ "Channel share trends"
2. **Kicker** (optional, ≤ 2 lines): the context or contrast that makes the title matter.
3. **Exhibits** (1–3, see `docs/exhibit_library.md`): each one proves the title. Highlight the one bar, cell or segment the title is about, and annotate the insight directly on the chart.
4. **Commentary** (2–4 short paragraphs or 3–5 bullets): why it happens, what is surprising, what it means for the buyer. Each paragraph opens with its claim.
5. **Takeaway bar** (one sentence, ≤ 160 chars): the "so what" for the buyer, an implication or an action. Never restate the title.

The test before a page ships: read only the title and the takeaway. They must say something different and both be worth knowing.

## 4. Hook techniques (use several per report, never the same twice in a row)

The examples show the shape only. They are illustrative; never reuse their wording or numbers without your own sourced evidence.

| Technique | How | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **The surprising number, scaled** | A number plus a comparison that makes it vivid | "One in three peso spent on packaged goods now goes through a store smaller than a parking space." |
| **The contradiction** | Two true facts that seem not to fit | "Modern trade is opening more stores than ever, and losing share." |
| **The stakes** | Money, time or position at risk, in the buyer's terms | "Get the distributor wrong and a launch loses its first 18 months." |
| **The concrete scene** | One specific, real-world image instead of an abstraction | "A sachet of coffee crosses four hands between the factory and a Cebu street." |
| **The reversal** | State the common belief, then overturn it with evidence | "The conventional route is modern trade first. The data points the other way." |
| **The named mechanism** | Give the pattern a short, memorable name and reuse it | "the showroom role", "the sachet ceiling" |
| **The countdown** | A dated trigger that creates urgency | "From 2027, packaging rules move costs onto the brand owner." |

## 5. Rules that keep it credible

- **Every hook must be true and sourced.** A hook with a weak number does more damage than no hook. Tag it like any other claim.
- **No hype words:** "game-changer", "unprecedented", "explosive", "massive". The surprise comes from the fact, not the adjective.
- **No question headlines** (voice_guide). Questions may appear in body text sparingly, and only when the next sentence answers them.
- **Concrete beats abstract:** a store, a pack price, a named rule, a named company's move.
- **Uncertainty is part of the story.** Say what we don't know and what would settle it; it builds trust.

## 6. Length

- **There is no page cap.** Give each analysis the pages its evidence earns, typically 1–3. A 30–40 page report is fine when every page passes the test in §3.
- **The only cut is the anti-padding rule:** drop any page that carries no new claim backed by data.
- **Character caps are physical fit, not content budgets.** If a page overflows, split it into two pages (a second exhibit, a deeper cut) rather than trimming the insight. Trim only filler.
