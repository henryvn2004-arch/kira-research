How we built this view

This view rests on two evidence streams: analyst research — English- and Burmese-language desk research, customs tables, licensing notices read through law-firm summaries, distributor and retailer disclosures — and secondary anchors — official trade statistics, partner-country trade data and multilateral releases. Every quantitative claim carries a source tag.

Analyst research

→ Customs series, read with care
Ministry of Commerce tables by fiscal year, goods group, border station and origin. Official tables stop in mid-2024; later years are our estimates and are labelled as such.
→ Rule readings
Licensing, tariff and labelling notices checked against two or more independent summaries (law firms, US and Japanese trade offices).
→ Analyst synthesis
Sizing of imported FMCG, the 2030 scenarios, pocket scores and option ratings. Tagged inline as [Kira estimates].

Secondary anchors

→ Partner-country data
Supplier-side trade data reported by the US agricultural attaché and Thai trade bodies, used to test how far Myanmar records understate arrivals.
→ Official and multilateral releases
World Bank, FATF, EU, US Treasury and UK sanctions notices, cited by named source.
→ Company disclosures
Distributor, retailer and brand-owner announcements for who operates, and how, in 2025-2026.

The tension: Thailand shipped 25% more farm and food products to Myanmar in 2025, while Myanmar's own import record kept shrinking.

Why the answer is "partly"

Shoppers have not walked away. Thailand supplied US$1.5 bn of farm and food products in 2025, up from US$1.2 bn in 2024 [USDA FAS Jun 2026], and premium demand persists in Yangon [USDA FAS Jun 2026].

Supply is rationed by rule. Since January 2026 an importer may file one licence per product code a month [Tilleke Jan 2026], and must match imports with its own export earnings [USDA FAS Aug 2025].

The kyat sets the price. The market rate runs 1.7-1.8 times the official 2,100 per dollar [CBM Jun 2026], so imported goods are priced at the street rate.

Enter through a licensed national distributor with dairy and ingredient lines first; hold consumer-brand launches behind licence and FATF gates.

What the record shows, and where it stops

Border goods fell fastest. The border share of consumer imports dropped from 25.5% in FY2020-21 to 16.1% in FY2023-24 and 9.6% in the first quarter of FY2024-25 [MoC 2024].

Then the published tables end. By 19 July 2024 consumer goods imports were down 48% on the same weeks a year earlier [MoC Jul 2024]. Total imports reached US$12.5 bn in FY2024-25 [USDA FAS Aug 2025]; at a 21-26% consumer share we put the year at US$2.6-3.2 bn [Kira estimates].

No volume series: customs publishes values only; volumes cannot be tracked from official data.
Plan on a recorded consumer import pool near US$3 bn, not the US$4.7 bn of FY2022-23, and on border routes that open and close.

We put imported FMCG at about US$1.5 bn in 2025, a little over half of recorded consumer goods imports

The customs "articles of use" group also holds vehicles, appliances, medicines and palm oil; FMCG is the packaged slice.

From imported food and drink to imported FMCG
Myanmar · US$ bn · 2023 base from supplier-reported data, then our adjustments
Source: [USDA FAS Aug 2024]; [Kira estimates]

How we sized it

Start from what suppliers report. Consumer-oriented food and drink imports reached US$1.7 bn in 2023 [USDA FAS Aug 2024]; this counts fresh fruit, meat and seafood as well as packaged goods.

Then adjust both ways. We take out 25-35% for fresh and chilled items and add US$0.3-0.5 bn of soap, detergent, toothpaste and cosmetics, giving US$1.4-1.8 bn in 2023 [Kira estimates]. Applying the fall in recorded consumer imports, partly offset by rising Thai supply, gives US$1.3-1.7 bn in 2025 [Kira estimates].

What would change it: a published HS-level customs table for FY2024-25 or FY2025-26.
A US$1.5 bn pool is small for a region, but it is concentrated in a few categories and a few distributors, which makes it reachable.

What the gap does to an import

Imports are priced at the market rate. Importers who earn their own dollars or buy them in the market pay 3,600-3,900 kyat each [CBM Jun 2026]; retail prices follow the market, not the official 2,100.

Volatility is the bigger cost. The rate swung from ~3,900 in 2023 to ~6,000 in 2024 [RFA Aug 2023] [USDA FAS Aug 2024]. When Myawaddy trade stalled in early 2024, local goods prices doubled [RFA Burmese Jan 2024].

Watch: any move to unify the official and market rates would reset every price list in a week.
Quote in dollars or baht to the importer and let the distributor carry kyat pricing; a fixed kyat shelf price will not survive a quarter.

Border routes are fast and cheap when open; since 2024 they have been the first casualty of fighting and policy.

Route and region

The gate opens and shuts with the conflict. Myanmar closed the Myawaddy checkpoint on 18 August 2025 and reopened it on 28 May 2026 [ANN Jun 2026]; Thai border trade with Myanmar fell 7.4% in 2025 [Thai DFT 2025].

Sea is slower but steadier. Most sea cargo transits Laem Chabang; sea licences take three to five months against about one month at the border [USDA FAS Jun 2026] [USDA FAS Aug 2025]. Mandalay has 2,118 restaurants, Yangon 1,912 [USDA FAS Jun 2026].

Design the supply chain for sea freight into Yangon with Thai border trucking as an upside, not the base case.

A signal more than a cost

Five food groups pay more. Bakery products now pay 20% instead of 15%, cheese 10% instead of 5% [USDA FAS May 2026]; wine stays at 30% and spirits at 60% [USDA FAS Jun 2026].

How concentrated is supply, really?

Licensed supply is concentrated. Licences, export earnings and daily caps favour a few large importers [USDA FAS Aug 2025].

Unlicensed supply is not. Grey imports through border towns serve the mass tier; we cannot size them reliably and do not add them to our estimate.
